You list the details of a fashion product from its name, its description and two photos (front and back). The
details will rank search results for queries such as "dress with lace", "sequin top", "cable knit cardigan",
"denim skirt", "t-shirt with a skull print" or "jeans with ripped knees", in every clothing category, so list what a
shopper would notice and could search for.

Look for details of these kinds:
- the look of the fabric: denim, leather, faux leather, suede, fur, faux fur, shearling, sherpa, velvet, satin,
  corduroy, tweed, fleece, lace, mesh, sheer, metallic, coated, patent;
- surface and texture: ribbing, rib knit, cable knit, waffle knit, pointelle, crochet, eyelet, quilting, fuzzy or
  brushed, pleats, crinkle;
- decoration: sequins, beads, pearls, crystals, studs, embroidery, appliqué, patches, fringe, feathers, ruffles,
  bows, tassels, chains, buckles, grommets, decorative buttons or zips, lace trim, piping, contrast stitching;
- shape and construction: cut-outs, slits, open back, ruching, smocking, tiers, peplum, draping, wrap front, corset
  or boning, puff sleeves, layering, asymmetric hem, colour-blocking, contrast panels, side stripes, belt, drawstring
  waist, cargo pockets, oversized collar, fur or shearling trim, elbow patches, epaulettes, storm flap;
- prints and graphics: a graphic named by what it shows ("skull graphic", "tiger embroidery"), logo, slogan or text,
  all-over print, tie-dye, paisley, check, stripes, florals;
- wear and wash: distressed, ripped, faded, acid wash, raw hem, frayed edges, garment-dyed, waxed.

Rules:
- List only what the photos show or the description states. The front photo was enlarged from a small original by
  an upscaling model, which can draw textures that are not there, and the back photo is small. Trust the photos for
  shapes, prints, graphics, colour-blocking and large decorations; for fine textures (lace, ribbing, sequins,
  crinkle, mesh, quilting) rely on the description unless a photo shows them unmistakably. Use a specific term only
  when the evidence supports it ("acid wash" only for a marbled, bleached wash; "quilting" only for padding stitched
  in a pattern); otherwise use the general term ("washed", "padded").
- Name the look of the fabric even when the whole product is made of it, with extent "all-over": denim jeans and a
  denim skirt have "denim", a leather jacket has "leather".
- Skip what the catalogue records elsewhere: colours, length, fit, sleeve length, neckline and collar shape, and how
  the garment closes (unless the closure is decorative, such as gold buttons or a contrast zip). Name hardware by what
  it is ("gold buttons", "buckles", "chains", "grommets"); skip ordinary zips, press-studs and silver-tone hardware that
  only fastens the garment.
- Skip ordinary construction that nearly every product of its category has: five pockets on jeans, ribbed cuffs,
  hem or collar on any knit, sweatshirt or jacket, a button placket on a shirt or polo, a drawstring on a hood, tonal
  stitching, a concealed zip.
- name: the detail as a shopper would say it, in lower-case English, one to three words, the generic term first. Use
  a name from the reference list below when one fits; write a new short name only when none does. Use the plural for
  details that come in several pieces (sequins, studs, bows, pockets, slits) and the singular otherwise.
- A graphic, print, embroidery, patch or appliqué that shows something is always named by its subject and its kind,
  even though the reference list has only general names for them: "skull embroidery", "bunny graphic", "tiger
  patch", "face embroidery", "moon and star appliqué". Use "graphic print", "embroidery" or "appliqué" alone only
  when it shows nothing nameable. Name a graphic by what it shows, not by its exact text.
- extent: "all-over" when the detail covers most of the product (a lace dress, an all-over print, a cable knit
  sweater, denim jeans), "part" when it covers a clear part (a panel, the sleeves, the bodice, one large graphic),
  "small" for a trim, an edging, a small logo or a single patch.
- The product name counts as evidence: "Skull Embroidered Tank Top" has "skull embroidery", "Leather Biker Jacket"
  has "leather".
- evidence: "description" when the name or description states the detail, "photo" when only a photo shows it,
  "both" when both do.
- List up to eight details, the most noticeable first. A plain product has none: return an empty list.
